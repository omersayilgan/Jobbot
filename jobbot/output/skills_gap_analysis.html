5. Eligibility and language — read before drawing conclusions

Your German is not what is holding these roles back

1 of 96 of these roles ask for fluent or C1 German. The Munich deep-tech and aerospace employers you rank well against operate in English, and several say so explicitly. B1 is not the barrier here that it is often assumed to be, and it should not stop you applying to anything on your shortlist.

The honest caveat: that result partly reflects what this tool can see. Employers with public job-board APIs skew toward English-speaking scale-ups; the Mittelstand and public-sector employers who do require German are largely invisible to it. So read this as “German is not gating the roles on your list”, not as “German does not matter in Munich”. Improving it still widens the market beyond what this document covers.

The clearance clause at Isar Aerospace

Isar Aerospace attaches the same sentence to every posting: “Due to security clearance requirements, affiliations with countries listed under § 13 para. 1 no. 17 SÜG may affect the application process.” SÜG is the German Security Clearance Act. The list of states it refers to is maintained by the Federal Ministry of the Interior and is not public, so nothing here tells you whether it applies to you.

Two things follow. First, the same adverts state plainly that all qualified applicants are encouraged to apply, and they do not prioritise nationality. Second, this is a question for Isar's recruiters, not for guesswork — ask them directly rather than withdrawing on an assumption. You have already applied to several of their roles, which is the right move.

Airbus differs: its Munich-area adverts require suitability for an erweiterte Sicherheitsüberprüfung and very good written and spoken German. Those are stated conditions rather than ambiguous ones.

Mentioned too rarely to prioritise: EU/German citizenship (1), AUTOSAR (3), FPGA / HDL (4), MISRA / static analysis (1), German (fluent) (1) — named by fewer than five roles each, which is too thin an evidence base to act on.

6. How this was measured

  • Document frequency, not word frequency. A posting naming Python nine times counts once. Raw word counts would have made recruiting boilerplate the top “skill” in this analysis.
  • Employer reach is tracked separately. Helsing alone writes roughly 40% of the best-fit postings, so its house requirements — Rust, reinforcement learning, distributed systems — would otherwise read as market-wide demand. Section 2 exists to separate the two.
  • Importance = 45% share of roles + 35% share of employers + 20% average fit of the roles asking. A skill demanded by your strongest matches outranks a commoner one demanded by weak matches.
  • Levels are judgements, not measurements. They come from your CV, the TUM Leistungsnachweis and the METU transcript. Edit skills_taxonomy.yaml and re-run python3 run.py skills as they change.
  • Limits. Only employers with a public job-board API are covered, and a skill absent from an advert is not always absent from the job.
